Image of Jupiter taken by the Hubble Space Telescope

The Giant: Juipter


Jupiter is the fifth planet in the solar system. It has twice as much mass as all the other planets in the solar system. More than 1000 Earths can be fit inside the planet.


It is nearly a perfect sphere as:

  • Polar diameter: 133,708km
  • Equatorial diameter: 139,820km

Doing the math, its polar diameter is 6,000km less than its equatorial diameter


It rotates 45,000km per hour and consequently, it rotates on its axis every 10 hours.

The top layer of clouds is composed of ammonia. Beneath, in the middle deck, ammonia hydrosulfide crystals give Jupiter its dark red colour. The bottom deck is made out of frozen water, because the temperature is well below 0 Celsius there.

Beneath the atmospheric layer comes a layer of molecular hydrogen. As the pressure increases, the molecular hydrogen becomes metallic. The metallic hydrogen layer surrounds the hot silicate core.


The Great Red Spot

The Great Red Spot


The Great Red Spot is a phenomenon of Jovian weather. It rotates anticlockwise every 11 days. It is across an area of ~24,000km in length and ~12,000km in width. It has twice the diameter of Earth. It is composed of swirling clouds of primarily hydrogen and helium, along with ammonia and other gases. Its red colour is of mystery. It's probably because of the reaction of phosphorus with sunlight. It is at least 8km above the surrounding cloud layers.

Shoemaker-Levy 9

The Lasting Impacts of Comet Shoemaker-Levy 9

Shoemaker-Levy 9 was a fragmented comet that famously collided with Jupiter in July 1994, providing scientists with a rare and dramatic opportunity to study both a comet impact and the gas giant's atmosphere. Discovered in 1993 by astronomers Carolyn and Eugene Shoemaker and David Levy, the comet had been captured by Jupiter's gravity and torn apart into multiple pieces during a previous close approach. As the fragments slammed into Jupiter's southern hemisphere at speeds of around 60 km/s, they left behind massive, dark scars in the atmosphere, some larger than Earth. These impacts released huge amounts of energy and gave researchers valuable insights into Jupiter's atmospheric composition and dynamics, as well as the potential threat of comet impacts in our solar system.
